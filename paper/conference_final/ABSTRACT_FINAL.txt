Next-day air quality index (AQI) forecasts are usually judged by mean error, yet the decisions they inform depend on which days and places the errors fall. We ask whether an acceptable average conceals failures in geographic transfer, severe pollution, prediction intervals and warnings. Using Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) measurements distributed by OpenAQ, we evaluate forecasts of a three-pollutant CPCB-style AQI reconstructed from PM2.5, PM10 and ozone, issued at 20:00 Indian Standard Time for the following day under a rule that admits only information available before issue. Twelve expanding chronological folds supply 38,382 exact station-day targets and 363 Severe outcomes known only as lower bounds, from 171 stations in 97 cities. A Ridge model driven by forecast weather lowers mean absolute error from 52.82 to 43.86 AQI units against climatology, with a paired 95 percent interval for the reduction of 5.61 to 12.49, but adding measurement history does not establish an advantage over persistence. Withholding whole regions raises error by 2.18 units overall and by 9.97 units on Poor-or-worse days. Nominal 90 percent conformal intervals cover 87.6 percent of exact outcomes but 8.7 percent of Very Poor ones, and no interval reaches the Severe lower bound. Warning from an upper quantile cuts under-warning from 27.1 to 14.2 percent while raising over-warning from 24.7 to 41.7 percent. The operational atmospheric-composition correction is incomplete and the final 90-day test remains sealed.
